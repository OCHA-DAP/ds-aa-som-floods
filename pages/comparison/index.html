The open multi-model consensus against one model per river-season window, scored year by year on the same corrected benchmark, plus how much warning each one actually gives. August 2026.

Two corrections were applied before this comparison, on the branch fix/corrected-benchmark-and-lag. Neither design's published page has been modified, and nothing on the branch has been merged. The corrections are (1) the benchmark's return levels were being fitted on 2000–2026 while crossings were counted only to 2023, letting three years of post-window data decide the labels, and (2) the best-lag correlation search shifted the gauge series by row position rather than by calendar days, which forced almost every reported lag to zero. Both designs are scored here on the corrected benchmark, so the numbers below differ from those on either page. The headline: at envelope level, almost nothing changes

Both designs release the money in 8 of 25 years, a return period of 1-in-3.2, and both catch every one of the seven severe years in the corrected benchmark. On the two measures the framework is actually sized on — how often it pays out, and whether it pays out when the rivers are at their worst — the open multi-model design and the one-model-per-window design are indistinguishable.

They differ on which eighth year they spend. The open design activates in 2010, a year the benchmark does record as a flood. The single-model design activates in 2013, which it does not. So the open design ends the backtest with no activation outside the benchmark at all, and the single-model design with one. As the next section shows, that difference is not caused by the mixing rule — it comes entirely from GEOGloWS being in one design and not the other.

Reading the disagreements. The two designs put their votes in different places even where the envelope agrees. The open design counts (station, model) pairs over the five gauges that still report, so a single station can vote more than once; the single-model design counts points across all seven reporting-era gauges with one product per window. That is why the pool sizes and the counts are not directly comparable cell by cell — what is comparable is whether the window fired.

Isolating the change: it is GEOGloWS, not the mixing rule

The comparison above moves two things at once. The open design carries GEOGloWS in three of its four windows; the single-model design carries it in none, because the working group excluded it. So the table is not a clean test of “mixing models versus one per window”.

To separate them, the open design was rebuilt under its own rules — (station, model) pairs on the five still-reporting gauges, the −3 day timing guard, the 0.10 ρ relative floor, joint basin calibration — but with GEOGloWS excluded. The result is unambiguous:

Matched on providers, the two designs produce the same activation record. Open multi-model without GEOGloWS activates in 2006, 2013, 2014, 2016, 2018, 2019, 2020 and 2023 — the same eight years as the single-model design, including the same 2013 activation outside the benchmark — at the same 1-in-3.2 and with the same 7 of 7 severe coverage, and its median warning is the same 1 day late. Whether a window mixes products or runs on one turns out to make no difference at all to what the trigger does. Every difference in the year-by-year table above is attributable to GEOGloWS being in one design and not the other.
Which puts the weight on the GEOGloWS decision, not the WFP one. Including GEOGloWS is what removes the 2013 false activation. It is also what makes the open design look slow on lead time — but that reading is an artifact: GEOGloWS has no forecast archive before July 2024, so those units are scored on the retrospective at lead 0, and they are what the count waits for. Against the reason the working group excluded it — its forecasts run at 0.89× its own retrospective, so a level set as 1-in-5 behaves like a median 1-in-13 on the live forecast, with only two years of archive to fit a correction on — one avoided false activation in 25 years is a thin argument for keeping it. The exclusion looks right, and the mixing rule turns out to be free either way.

For every window activation, the earliest date the trigger condition is met on the historical forecasts, against the date the reference gauge first crossed its own 1-in-3 level that season. Positive means the trigger fired before the river got high; negative means it fired after. Neither design is reliably ahead of the river. On the common ≤ 6-day band the single-model design's median activation lands 1 day after the reference gauge crosses its 1-in-3 level; the open design without GEOGloWS lands at the same 1 day after. The apparent 7-day gap for the open design with GEOGloWS is an artifact of its lead-0 stand-ins, not a property of mixing models. The number that should set expectations with partners is the one both designs share: the action leg fires at roughly the same time the river gets high, ahead of the gauge in only about half of events. That is what the readiness leg exists to compensate for.
And the comparison flatters the open design. Three of its four windows include GEOGloWS units, which have no forecast archive before July 2024 and are therefore evaluated on the retrospective at lead 0 — hindsight, not a forecast. Juba Gu carries one such unit, Juba Deyr and Shabelle Deyr two each. Those units cannot be late, so the open design's lead times are an upper bound on what it would really deliver. The single-model design uses no stand-ins in the adopted configuration.

What this means for the decision

  • The move to one model per window costs nothing at all. Matched on providers, it produces the identical activation record to the open design: the same eight years, the same 1-in-3.2, the same 7 of 7 severe coverage and the same median lead time. Given the constraint came from WFP rather than from the data, that is the useful finding — the requirement can be honoured with nothing given up.
  • The real decision was GEOGloWS. Every difference between what was originally proposed and what is now proposed traces to excluding it. It buys back one false activation in 25 years, against thresholds that cannot be transferred to its live forecasts and only two years of archive to fix them with. On that trade the exclusion looks right.
  • Neither design is validated on lead time where it matters most. Both Deyr legs rest on GloFAS v5, which has no reforecast at all, so their operational behaviour is inferred from v4. That gap dominates the difference between the two designs.
