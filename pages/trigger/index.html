Somalia Riverine Flood Trigger / trigger analysis

The multi-source trigger mechanism

Proposed trigger for anticipatory action against riverine flooding on the Juba and Shabelle: a station consensus across GloFAS, Google Flood Hub and GEOGloWS, calibrated against SWALIM river gauges. August 2026.

1-in-3.7 overall action return period (either basin)
1-in-4.3 per basin, equal for Juba and Shabelle
7 / 7 activation years are documented major floods
7–12 d readiness lead time (action at ≤ 6 days)

The mechanism at a glance

The trigger is fully forecast-based. SWALIM's river-gauge record is the ground truth the mechanism is calibrated and validated against — it is not a trigger input. Each river basin (Juba, Shabelle) carries one specific trigger per flood season (Gu, April–June rains arriving March–May on the rivers; Deyr, October–December), and each specific trigger is staged:

  • Readiness (leads 7–12 days) — GloFAS ensemble-median forecast; releases only the small mobilisation share. GloFAS is the only provider whose forecasts reach past 7 days with a historical archive to validate against.
  • Action (leads ≤ 6 days) — the multi-source station consensus: the basin's 8 best-skill stations, each with its own return-period threshold, and the trigger activates when at least N of them exceed their thresholds on the same forecast valid day, within a single forecast issuance.

Backtested on 1999–2023, each basin activates 6 of 25 years (1-in-4.3, equal by construction), and at least one basin activates in 7 years — overall return period 3.7 years, meeting the ≥ 3-year specification. The activation years are 2006, 2013, 2016, 2018, 2019, 2020 and 2023 — every one a documented major flood year in Somalia. Per-basin thresholds are nominal (~1-in-4.3–5); the final adjustment belongs to the framework working group.

How the stations were chosen

Every combination of the 18 monitoring points and the three providers competes on one metric: best-lag Spearman rank correlation between the provider's reanalysis/retrospective discharge and the SWALIM reference-gauge level (Belet Weyne for the Shabelle, Luuq for the Juba), per river and season — the correlations computed in P. Wairimu's notebook 06, against the SWALIM benchmark her notebook 01 established (post-2000 record, official Moderate/High risk levels) and her notebook 05 showed must be primary over FloodScan on the Shabelle. One source per station — consensus should be spatial agreement, not the same water seen through two models — with a timing guard: among sources within 0.05 of a station's best correlation, the one whose signal arrives earliest wins.

A reading trap this figure used to invite: in Juba Deyr a GEOGloWS dot (Dollow, ρ 0.83 at lag +1) sits above-right of several GloFAS dots — but those are other stations. Following Dollow's own grey line: GloFAS v5 at Dollow is ρ 0.87 at lag +5, better on both axes; and the same holds at every Juba Deyr station (GloFAS beats GEOGloWS on ρ and lag at all ten). The outcome is a mechanism that is multi-source by competition, not by quota: Google Flood Hub carries the Gu windows, GloFAS the Deyr windows, and GEOGloWS is a documented watch item (it has no reforecast archive to validate against, and its forecasts run 0.6–0.7× its retrospective climatology, so retrospective-fitted thresholds would under-fire operationally).

Seasonal peaks: model vs gauge

Daily rank correlation can flatter a model that merely tracks the seasonal cycle. The sharper diagnostic is one point per season-year: the model's seasonal peak against the observed seasonal peak at the reference gauge — does the model rank the years correctly? For display each model's peak is divided by its own 1-in-6 threshold (rank correlation is unaffected by the normalisation), so the quadrants read directly: top-right = the model ran over its own threshold in a year the gauge also ran high.

Thresholds and calibration

Each selected station gets its threshold from its own model's climatology: the Weibull plotting position of its seasonal maxima (1999–2023) at the target return period. Magnitude bias between models cancels out — each station is equally likely to exceed its threshold by construction. The same principle carries into operations: we assume bias between reanalysis and forecast for every source, so a threshold is only ever applied to the product whose own historical values it was fitted on (the reforecast backtests below refit thresholds on the reforecast's climatology for exactly this reason). To inspect the indicators themselves, year by year, use the indicator explorer. The basin-level search then chooses, per season, the per-station return period and the consensus count N, subject to: per-basin activation frequency in the 1-in-4.3–6.5 band; maximum coverage of the basin's severe (1-in-5) benchmark years; fewest activations outside the moderate (1-in-3) benchmark set; and, among ties, a consensus fraction near 75% — a spatial-noise filter no single station can dominate, robust to one gauge failing.

Would it have worked operationally?

The calibration record is reanalysis — a model's afterwards-view of its own past. The operational test replays the actual historical forecasts: Google's reforecast (2016–2023) for the Gu legs, and for the GloFAS v5 Deyr legs the v4 reforecast (2003–2023) with thresholds refit on v4's own climatology, since no v5 reforecast exists. A leg activates operationally when a single forecast issuance shows the consensus at leads ≤ 6 days. (That forecast skill barely decays from lead 1 to 7 — it is initial-condition-driven on these slow rivers — is P. Wairimu's notebook 03 finding, and is what makes both the ≤ 6-day band and the 7–12-day readiness band viable at all.)

The one caveat that matters most. At ≤ 6-day leads the GloFAS v4 reforecast misses Deyr 2023 on the Shabelle — the largest flood in the record — even though the v5 reanalysis flags it clearly. The operational forecast has been v5 since August 2026 and should behave like the v5 reanalysis here, but there is no v5 reforecast to prove it. Re-verifying both Deyr legs the moment EWDS publishes a v5 reforecast is the single most important follow-up.

The readiness leg (7–12 days)

GloFAS ensemble-median forecasts at leads 7–12 days (the leads-8-12 reforecast was downloaded specifically for this band), same station sets, thresholds on GloFAS v4's own climatology, at a lower bar — readiness releases only the mobilisation share and may activate more often than action. It fully covers the action years for Juba Gu, Juba Deyr and Shabelle Gu at 1-in-3.1–4.4 frequencies. Shabelle Deyr readiness covers only 1 of 3 action years — at 7–12 days GloFAS v4 cannot see Deyr-2023-type events at all. Individually the readiness legs sit at 1-in-3.1–4.4, but their union runs ~1-in-1.7 — the working group should see that number explicitly when deciding the mobilisation share.

For each basin, whether each indicator's consensus would have activated on the calibration record (that season's station set and N-of-8 requirement, with the indicator's own 1-in-6 thresholds — GEOGloWS included, via its retrospective; G = Gu, D = Deyr), alongside the SWALIM benchmark class, EM-DAT flood impacts, IDMC flood displacement and CERF flood allocations.

Coverage & caveats. EM-DAT is a floor (entry-criteria bias), split to seasons by start month (Mar–Jun → Gu, Sep–Dec → Deyr) and to basins by keywords in the event's location text (J = Juba, S = Shabelle; blank = not attributable — often flash floods elsewhere). IDMC displacement (2010–2025) is attributed to basins from the region in the event name — region tagging only becomes systematic around 2023, so earlier riverine displacement mostly sits in "other / not attributed" (e.g. the 406k displaced in Deyr 2019, largely Belet Weyne). CERF amounts are national flood allocations from the OneGMS mirror, windowed by allocation date, basins from the narrative summaries. Impact columns are shown for context, not as a skill score: a formal skill-vs-impact statistic still requires choosing an impact threshold.
